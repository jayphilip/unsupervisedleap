Somewhere around 350 BCE, Plato wrote that philosophy is a preparation for death. Most readers take this as Plato being gloomy. I think he was being practical.

Here's the information: you are going to die. So is everyone you love. So is everyone who will ever read this sentence.

You already knew that. You've known it since you were old enough to understand what it meant. And yet — somehow — it's the piece of information most of us spend the most energy not thinking about.

## The Stoic Hack

The Stoics had a practice called *memento mori* — "remember that you will die." Not as a morbid exercise, but as a tool for clarity. Marcus Aurelius, running an empire, still found time to remind himself daily that he was temporary.

The logic is elegant: if your time is finite, then how you spend it is the most consequential decision you make. Everything else is downstream of that.

The hack isn't to be sad about mortality. The hack is to let mortality do the prioritization work you've been avoiding.

## What It Actually Changes

I used to think memento mori was a thought experiment for people with too much time. Then I had a kid.

There is something about becoming responsible for a new life that makes your own finitude suddenly, viscerally real. It's not morbid — it's orienting. The trivialities that used to consume my attention started to look different when held up against the question: *is this what I want to have been doing?*

Not in a paralyzing way. In a clarifying one.

## The Question Worth Asking

Philosophy at its best isn't about finding answers. It's about getting the questions right.

"What should I do today?" is a fine question. But it lives downstream of a better one: *"What kind of life am I building, given that I have a finite amount of time to build it?"*

You already have the most important piece of information. The rest is just deciding what to do with it.

Plato wasn't being gloomy. He was being useful.
