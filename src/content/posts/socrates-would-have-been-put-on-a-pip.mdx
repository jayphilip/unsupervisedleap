There is a specific person in every organization. You know them. They are the one who, when the VP finishes explaining the new strategy, raises their hand and asks: "But what problem are we actually solving?" Not hostile. Genuinely curious. The room goes slightly cold.

They ask it in the all-hands. They ask it in the planning meeting. They ask it one-on-one with their manager, who starts noting it in a document. They mean well. They are usually right. They almost always get managed out.

Agnes Callard's *Open Socrates* is a book about that person. Except the person is Socrates, the organization is Athens, and the hemlock was the severance.

---

## The Gadfly Was Annoying on Purpose

Socrates described himself, in Plato's *Apology*, as a gadfly sent by the gods to sting Athens — a great but sluggish horse that would otherwise drift into comfortable, unexamined sleep. The sting was not a side effect. It was the mission. Callard quotes Socrates reporting, with apparent surprise, "I realized, to my sorrow and alarm, that I was getting unpopular." He kept going anyway.

Callard's reading of this is precise and, I think, underappreciated: Socrates was not accidentally irritating. He was not a contrarian with poor social skills who happened to ask good questions. The irritation *was* the service. The discomfort was not a side effect of the method, it was the method.

This is Callard's central move in *Open Socrates*. Socrates is not a historical figure to be catalogued and set aside. He is not a set of clever rhetorical tricks. He is a practice and the practice is available to anyone willing to pay the costs.

The practice is simple to describe and very hard to do: find someone who claims to know what virtue is, what good leadership looks like, what fairness means, what success requires and ask them to explain it. Follow their explanation wherever it goes. When it contradicts itself, note the contradiction. Don't assert a better answer. Just keep asking.

Athens killed him for it. He kept doing it until they did.

## The Infrastructure of Performed Certainty

The modern professional world has built an elaborate infrastructure to prevent exactly this.

We call it thought leadership. We call it executive presence. We call it having a strong point of view. We reward the person who walks into a room and projects confidence, who has a framework, a take, a slide deck that makes the complexity legible and the decision feel obvious.

This is not entirely wrong. Organizations need decisions. Meetings need to end. Paralysis is a real failure mode.

But something gets lost in that infrastructure. The thing that gets lost is the move Callard's Socrates makes before any inquiry begins: the acknowledgment that you don't actually know what virtue is.

Moral ignorance, in Callard's reading, is not pessimism. It is not relativism. It is the intellectually honest starting point for anyone who wants to actually get things right rather than merely perform getting things right.

The thought leader starts from certainty and works backward to evidence. Socrates starts from acknowledged ignorance and works forward — slowly, painfully, often without arriving anywhere satisfying, toward something more real.

The infrastructure rewards the first posture. It punishes the second.

## The Three Things That Happen to the Gadfly

You can observe this pattern in any organization that has been running long enough.

First, the questioning gets reframed. "She doesn't have a bias for action." "He can't take a position." "They're always playing devil's advocate." The move here is to reclassify intellectual honesty as a personality deficiency. The person isn't wrong to question, they just lack the courage or the competence to decide.

Second, they get isolated. Decisions start getting made before they're in the room. The early alignment conversations, the informal hallway consensus — these happen without them. By the time the meeting occurs, the question has already been answered. Raising it now would be disruptive. So they raise it anyway, which confirms the diagnosis.

Third, the organization does what Athens did. Not the drama of a hemlock trial...something quieter. A restructuring that lands on their role. A culture-fit conversation. A performance improvement plan with objectives that were never quite achievable. It is rarely punitive in intent. That is the disturbing part. The organization genuinely believes it is making a reasonable decision.

Athens thought the same thing.

---

## Callard's Answer: It's Still Worth It

Here is what I expected Callard to do at this point in *Open Socrates*: offer a practical path. Show how to be the gadfly without getting stung. Soften the edges.

She doesn't.

Her argument is not that the examined life is comfortable, or strategically advisable, or that you can preserve your career while practicing it. Her argument is that the examined life (Socrates' phrase) is specifically organized around being shown you are wrong. Repeatedly. In front of other people. The discomfort is not a side effect to minimize. It is the practice itself.

The question this raises is not *how* to be Socrates without his fate. The question is whether you think the examination is worth it, knowing the costs in advance.

That is not a comfortable conclusion. I have been sitting with it since I finished the book this week.

## The Socratic Option Is Always Available

The gap between what everyone is saying and what everyone knows to be true is present in almost every meeting. Most people have learned to let it close on its own. Socrates never did. Callard's point is that this was not a personality defect. It was a practice. And it's still available.

---

## What I Don't Advise

Athens said no. Most organizations still do.

Socrates' last line in the *Laches*, after the conversation had collapsed without resolution: *"What I don't advise is that we remain as we are"* (Laches 201a).

I keep coming back to that. It's not an answer. It's the only move that's always available.
