Before I ran this experiment, I expected brandy to be the weird one — the BBC deep-dish recipe soaks sultanas in brandy, and I figured both would land far from everything else. They didn't land together. In ingredient embedding space, brandy migrates toward pastry and structure, while sultanas drift toward the spice and aromatic cluster. Neither landed where I'd have put them. That result says something true about how language works, and it's what this post is about.

I pulled twelve apple pie recipes from Food52, Bon Appétit, BBC Good Food, and AllRecipes, extracted their ingredients, embedded them locally with a BERT-family model, and projected to 2D with UMAP. The question: what clusters? Most of it is exactly what you'd expect. One thing isn't.

<DefinitionBlock
  term="How ingredient embeddings work"
  formal="A text embedding model maps each ingredient string to a vector in ℝ³⁸⁴. Vectors are trained such that semantically similar inputs have geometrically proximate representations — ingredients that appear in similar contexts in food writing end up near each other in the vector space."
  plain="The model has read an enormous amount of recipe text. It knows that 'cinnamon' and 'nutmeg' appear together constantly, so they land near each other. It doesn't know that 'brandy' is a fruit spirit — it only knows where that word appears in sentences. Which turns out to be nowhere near apples. It has never tasted anything. It has only read."
  source="BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5 via fastembed (Devlin et al., BERT, 2019)"
/>

UMAP then takes those 384-dimensional vectors and finds a 2D layout that preserves neighborhood structure — ingredients close in high-dimensional space stay close in the plot. With 121 points and `n_neighbors=5`, the clusters are suggestive, not definitive. This is a toy experiment. But the patterns are real enough to be interesting.

## The Embedding Visualization

121 ingredient-recipe pairs across 12 recipes (same ingredient appearing in multiple recipes shows up as multiple points, one per recipe). Each point is one normalized ingredient string, embedded with `BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5` (384 dimensions, ONNX, no API key) and reduced to 2D with UMAP using cosine distance. Hover to see the ingredient name and its recipe.

<CalloutBox type="info" title="How to read this plot">
Points that are close together have similar semantic embeddings — the model associates them with similar contexts in food writing. Tight clusters mean ingredients that play the same conceptual role across recipes.
</CalloutBox>

## What the Clusters Tell Us

The AllRecipes recipes are simpler — fewer ingredients, more generic strings — and they land deep inside existing clusters rather than forming new ones. "Easy Apple Pie" has six ingredients; all six land with the crowd. The simplest recipe is the most redundant, in embedding space.

### Apples and Citrus (top-center)

Every apple variant lands together at the top of the plot: Granny Smith, Golden Delicious, Macintosh, Bramley, "tart apples," "sweet apples," "sweet-tart apples." Lemon juice and lemon squeeze variants cluster along the left edge — citrus travels with fruit. Apple cider lands here too, grouped with fruit rather than with liquids despite functioning as a liquid in recipes.

### Eggs and Water (left)

Water, ice water, egg, egg whites, egg yolk, eggs — all liquid binders and plain water, pulled to the left band. Salt and kosher salt land nearby. Milk clusters here too: in baking prose, milk appears beside eggs far more often than beside cream or spices. This is a structurally coherent group — ingredients that do binding and seasoning work rather than flavoring or structural work.

### Spices and Aromatics (center-left)

Cinnamon, nutmeg, allspice, cardamom, cardamom pods, ground pepper, and salt variants cluster in the center-left band — drawn from multiple recipes, independent of source. The model doesn't know which recipe they came from. It knows these words appear in near-identical contexts in food writing. They're interchangeable to the model in a way they're not to a baker.

The Food52 "Apple Pie" lists "ground allspice or cinnamon" as a single ingredient — the recipe literally treats them as alternatives. In embedding space, they're already neighbors. The recipe and the model agree.

Sultanas land here too. Dried fruit in recipe writing appears in sentences about flavoring and spice — not about fresh fruit. The sultanas traveled with the aromatics, not the apples.

### Butter and Dairy (center)

"Very cold unsalted butter," "unsalted butter," "butter," "vegetable shortening," "cornstarch" — fats and thickeners, grouped in the center of the plot. Cream, crème fraîche, and whipped cream sit at the edges of this zone — dairy fats adjacent to butter in embedding space, as they are in recipe writing.

### Sweeteners (bottom-left)

Granulated sugar, light brown sugar, dark brown sugar, golden caster sugar, white sugar, demerara, turbinado, coarse sugar — all pulled to the bottom-left. British golden caster sugar sits slightly offset from American granulated, reflecting a different semantic footprint in recipe corpora.

### Pastry, Dough, and Flour (right)

The right side is pastry territory: double-crust pastry references, shortcrust pastry, pie dough disks, all-purpose flour, plain flour, "quantity all-butter pie dough." Baking vessels — "deep pie dish," "tart pan with removable bottom" — land at the right edge too. The model has correctly identified baking vessels as belonging to the same structural context as pastry.

### Brandy (center-right, near pastry)

**Brandy** doesn't land with the apples. It doesn't land with the spices, or the sweeteners, or the eggs. It sits center-right, near the pastry and dough cluster — in the structural zone of the recipe rather than the flavoring zone.

This is what the experiment turns on. Brandy is a spirit distilled from fruit, and you might expect it near apples or citrus. Instead it migrates toward pastry and baking — language that describes what you do to construct something, not what you taste. Sultanas, meanwhile, drifted to the spice cluster: dried fruit appears beside aromatics and flavorings in recipe prose more often than beside fresh fruit.

Neither landed where intuition would place them. They landed where language puts them. The model has never tasted brandy. But it has read enough recipes to know which words appear in the same sentences.

## What This Actually Tells Us

The American vs. British divide shows up clearly too. The BBC recipes share Bramley apples, golden caster sugar, and plain flour — a very specific British pantry vocabulary. The American recipes use different words for the same things: "all-purpose flour" vs. "plain flour," "granulated sugar" vs. "caster sugar." In embedding space, these aren't quite the same point. The clusters partly reflect cuisine, not just chemistry.

The Grandma Ople and Chef John recipes share an unusual technique — a butter-sugar caramel sauce poured directly over the unbaked lattice crust. Neither Food52 nor Bon Appétit nor BBC uses this approach. But in ingredient embedding space, there's no separate cluster for it. The technique is invisible; only the ingredients leave a trace. "Brown sugar," "white sugar," "unsalted butter," and "water" all land exactly where you'd expect. The caramel sauce, as a structural idea, doesn't exist in the embedding.

Every ingredient in this dataset was sourced from a recipe written in English, by a food writer, for an audience with particular vocabulary and habits. The model learned from that writing. When it puts brandy near pastry instead of near apples, it's not hallucinating — it's reporting what English-language food writing implies. Which raises a question worth sitting with: if you trained the same model on French recipe corpora, or Japanese, would brandy still land near flour?

Language shapes what we see. Not the food.

## A Note on Method

1. Collected 12 recipes from Food52, Bon Appétit, BBC Good Food, and AllRecipes
2. Extracted ingredient strings, normalized out quantities and measurements
3. Embedded each unique ingredient using `BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5` via `fastembed` (384 dimensions, ONNX, fully local — no API key)
4. Reduced to 2D with UMAP (`cosine` metric, `n_neighbors=5`, `min_dist=0.2`) — UMAP uses cosine distance in the 384-dimensional space to build the neighborhood graph before projecting
5. Normalized coordinates to [-1, 1] and plotted

The normalization step strips quantities and measurements but isn't perfect — some ingredient strings in the Best-Ever recipe retain measurement prefixes ("tbsp", "tsp", "lb") because the scraper included them without whitespace separation. They still land in the right semantic neighborhoods, but their tooltip labels are ugly.

The scraper also occasionally includes equipment ("a diameter tart pan with removable bottom") or serving suggestions ("softly whipped cream to serve") as ingredient strings. These land in contextually adjacent clusters (equipment near pastry, cream near sweeteners) rather than as isolated outliers — which is itself revealing.
